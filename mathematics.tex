\section{Purpose and scope}

AlphaVerify asks a narrow question about a market condition: \emph{when the
condition holds, does price become more or less likely to reach a given
distance up or down within a given time, and is that change larger than
chance would produce?} It answers in five stages:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X l@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{ConceptPaper}
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Question} & \textbf{Result} \\
\midrule
1 Measure & How often was each barrier touched, over all dates and within each feature bin? & $B$, $Y_k$ \\
2 Compare & How much does a bin change those chances? & $Z_k=Y_k-B$ \\
3 Validate & Could a market without memory produce as large a change? & $\widehat p$ \\
4 Select & Which bins pass, and how many would pass by chance? & selected bins, $q$ \\
5 Combine & What do the passing conditions that hold today imply together? & $P$, $J$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
This document explains what each stage computes and why it is built that
way. Formulas are written at the level needed to follow the idea; the exact
numerical conventions are collected briefly in Section~\ref{sec:numerics}.

\topic{What the comparison establishes}

Validation compares each result with a fitted simulator in which prices have
no memory.
\begin{keyconcept}{What a small p-value means}
This simulator rarely produces a score as large as the observed one.
\end{keyconcept}
It does not by itself show out-of-sample performance, an edge after costs,
or a calibrated 5\% false-positive rate in real markets, which do have
memory (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

\section{Central concepts}\label{sec:surface}

\subsection{The Surface as the computational unit}

Everything in AlphaVerify is laid out on two ordered grids: a barrier grid
$\Delta$ and a horizon grid $\Theta$.
\begin{itemize}
 \item A \emph{barrier} $\delta\in\Delta$ is a distance from the starting
       date's close. For example, $\delta=-0.05$ is $5\%$ below that close,
       and $\delta=+0.05$ is $5\%$ above it.
 \item A \emph{horizon} $\theta\in\Theta$ is a number of bars ahead. For
       example, $\theta=10$ asks whether the barrier is touched at any point
       within the next ten bars, not only at the end of the tenth.
\end{itemize}
A Surface assigns a real number to every pair of a barrier and a horizon.
All Surfaces therefore live in one space:
\begin{keyconcept}{The Surface space}
\[
  \mathcal S=\mathbb R^{\Delta\times\Theta},
  \qquad F\in\mathcal S,
  \qquad F(\delta,\theta)\in\mathbb R.
\]
\end{keyconcept}
$\mathcal S$ is the space of Surfaces, and $F$ is one Surface. As an array
it has $|\Delta|$ rows and $|\Theta|$ columns, but mathematically the whole
grid is a single element of $\mathcal S$: one point in this space. That
element is the unit the pipeline operates on. It is measured, compared,
scored, and selected as one object, even though it is built cell by cell.

\topic{Blank cells}

A cell whose value would rest on fewer than 30 dates is left blank. A blank
cell has no value; it is not zero. A Surface keeps its full shape even when
some of its cells are blank. Formally, a partially supported Surface is an
element $F\in\mathcal S$ together with its set of supported cells
$\mathcal M\subseteq\Delta\times\Theta$; values of $F$ outside $\mathcal M$
are never used.

\subsection{Observed and synthetic histories}\label{sec:paths}

Everything is computed on a price history: a sequence of bars with open,
high, low, close, and volume. The observed history is the real market.
Stage~3 also builds $I=1{,}000$ synthetic histories
(Section~\ref{sec:null}) and runs each of them through exactly the same
measurement and scoring. Index the histories by $i=0,1,\ldots,I$, with $i=0$
the observed one. Because the rules are identical for every $i$, the formulas
in this document describe one history and leave $i$ out; it appears only
where observed and synthetic results are compared (Stage~3).

\subsection{The objects carried through the pipeline}

\begin{keyconcept}{Four Surface objects, one common space}
\[
  X_t,\quad Y_k,\quad B,\quad Z_k\in\mathcal S.
\]
\end{keyconcept}
Their subscripts say which member of $\mathcal S$ is meant, a date $t$ or a
bin $k$; their coordinates are always $(\delta,\theta)$. Their values mean
different things, but they are elements of the same space. On supported
cells:
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[Outcome Surface] $X_t(\delta,\theta)\in\{0,1\}$ is $1$ if barrier
       $\delta$ was touched within $\theta$ bars after date $t$, and $0$ if not.
 \item[Conditional probability Surface] $Y_k(\delta,\theta)\in[0,1]$ is the
       share of dates in feature bin $k$ that were followed by that touch.
       For example, $0.60$ means $60\%$ of them.
 \item[Baseline Surface] $B(\delta,\theta)\in[0,1]$ is the same share over
       all dates, whatever the feature says. There is one baseline per
       history, shared by every bin.
 \item[Shift Surface] $Z_k(\delta,\theta)=Y_k(\delta,\theta)-B(\delta,\theta)\in[-1,1]$
       is how much the bin changes the chance of the touch. $0.60$ against a
       baseline of $0.40$ gives $+0.20$, shown as $+20\%$: a 20-percentage-point
       difference, not a relative change.
\end{description}
$Y_k$ and $B$ are the same calculation over different groups of dates: the
dates in bin $k$, or all dates. The baseline is not one of the bins.

\subsection{The score}\label{sec:score-objects}

Stage~3 condenses each shift Surface into two scalars:
\begin{keyconcept}{One score and one support flag per bin}
\[
 Q_k\in\mathbb R_{\geq0},\qquad V_k\in\{0,1\}.
\]
\end{keyconcept}
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[Score] $Q_k$ measures how lopsided the shift is between barriers
       above and below the close. It is not a probability and can exceed one.
 \item[Support flag] $V_k=1$ when at least one cell of the bin can be
       scored. An unsupported bin is stored as $Q_k=0$, $V_k=0$ and is not
       tested; a supported bin can also score $Q_k=0$, with $V_k=1$.
\end{description}
\begin{keyconcept}{The central computation}
\normalsize
\[
 \{X_t\}_t\subset\mathcal S
 \xrightarrow{\text{touch rates}}
 Y_k,\ B\in\mathcal S
 \xrightarrow{\text{subtract baseline}}
 Z_k\in\mathcal S
 \xrightarrow{\text{score}}
 (Q_k,V_k)\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}\times\{0,1\}.
\]
\end{keyconcept}
Every step until scoring maps Surfaces to Surfaces. Scoring is the one step
that leaves $\mathcal S$: it turns a whole Surface into a single number and
its support flag.
A bin's score also depends on the other bins of the same feature: a cell
counts only where at least two bins have enough dates
(Section~\ref{sec:score}).

\begin{keyconcept}{The support threshold}
\[
  n_{\min}=30
\]
\normalsize A cell measured from fewer than $n_{\min}$ dates is blank: it
has no value, not a value of zero.
\end{keyconcept}
Counts shrink at longer horizons, because the last $\theta$ dates of a
history have no complete window ahead of them. Long horizons therefore go
blank first.

\section{Stage 1: measurement of probability surfaces}\label{sec:measurement}

Stage~1 turns prices into touch rates. It first records, for every date,
whether each barrier was reached, then groups the dates by the feature and
averages.

\begin{keyconcept}{Stage 1 at a glance}
\normalsize
\[
 \text{prices}
 \xrightarrow{\text{was it touched?}}
 X_t\ \text{for every date}
 \xrightarrow{\text{average by bin, and over all dates}}
 (Y_k,\ B).
\]
\end{keyconcept}

\subsection{From prices to an outcome Surface}

Start from the close on date $t$. Barrier $\delta$ sits at the price
$\Close_t\,(1+\delta)$. It is touched within $\theta$ bars when some later
low reaches it (a barrier below) or some later high reaches it (a barrier
above). ``Later'' means bars $t+1$ to $t+\theta$; bar $t$ itself never counts.
\begin{keyconcept}{The outcome Surface: was the barrier touched?}
\[
X_t(\delta,\theta)=
\begin{cases}
 \ind\Bigl[\displaystyle\min_{1\leq j\leq\theta}\Low_{t+j}\leq\Close_t(1+\delta)\Bigr], & \delta<0,\\[10pt]
 \ind\Bigl[\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq\theta}\High_{t+j}\geq\Close_t(1+\delta)\Bigr], & \delta\geq0,
\end{cases}
\qquad t\in\mathcal E_\theta,
\]
\normalsize where $\ind[\cdot]$ is $1$ when its statement is true and $0$
otherwise.
\end{keyconcept}
Highs and lows are used, not closes, so a barrier counts as touched even if
price comes back before the bar ends. The zero barrier asks whether any later
high reached the starting close. Only dates with a complete window count:
\[
 \mathcal E_\theta=\{t\in\mathcal T:\ \text{bar }t+\theta\text{ exists}\},
\]
so the last $\theta$ dates of a history are left out at horizon $\theta$.

$X_t$ depends only on prices. The feature never changes an outcome; it only
decides which dates are averaged together.

\subsection{From feature values to bins}

Bins are quantiles of the feature. With the default of $K=10$ bins, each bin
holds about a tenth of the dates that have a feature value, from the lowest
values to the highest. Dates without a feature value, such as during an
indicator's warm-up period, belong to no bin.

Precisely, the edges are the interior quantiles of the finite feature values,
\[
 e_j=\text{the }\tfrac{j}{K}\text{-quantile of }\{x_t\},\qquad j=1,\ldots,K-1,
\]
with repeated edges removed and only edges in $\bigl(\min x,\ \max x\bigr]$
kept. A date's bin is the number of edges strictly below its value, and a bin
is the set of dates assigned to it:
\begin{keyconcept}{Bin assignment}
\[
 k(t)=\#\{j:\ e_j<x_t\},\qquad
 A_k=\{t:\ x_t\text{ is finite and }k(t)=k\}.
\]
\end{keyconcept}
A value exactly on a bin edge is not strictly above it, so it goes to the
lower bin. With edges $2$ and $5$:
\[
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
 x & 2 & 3 & 5 & 6\\ \hline
 \text{bin} & \heatcell{ConceptPaper}{0} & \heatcell{ConceptInk!18!white}{1}
   & \heatcell{ConceptInk!18!white}{1} & \heatcell{ConceptInk!36!white}{2}
\end{array}
\]
A heavily tied feature can end up with fewer bins than requested, because
repeated values produce repeated edges, which merge. Asking for four bins
from 60 zeros and 20 ones gives the quantiles $(0,0,0.25)$; only $0.25$
survives, so there are two bins.

\subsection{Averaging outcome surfaces}\label{sec:averaging}

A touch rate is the share of a group of dates that were followed by a touch.
For any group of dates $A$, it defines a whole Surface:
\[
 \Pi[A](\delta,\theta)
 =\frac{1}{|A\cap\mathcal E_\theta|}\sum_{t\in A\cap\mathcal E_\theta}X_t(\delta,\theta),
 \qquad\text{blank when }|A\cap\mathcal E_\theta|<n_{\min}.
\]
$\Pi$ maps a group of dates to an element of $\mathcal S$. Stage~1 applies it
to two kinds of group:
\begin{keyconcept}{One touch rate, two groups of dates}
\[
 Y_k=\Pi[A_k],\qquad B=\Pi[\mathcal T].
\]
\normalsize In words: the share of dates in bin $k$, or of all dates, that
were followed by a touch. The dates behind a cell are
$N_k(\theta)=|A_k\cap\mathcal E_\theta|$ and $N(\theta)=|\mathcal E_\theta|$.
\end{keyconcept}
Both count only dates with $\theta$ bars of future data, and both are blank
below $n_{\min}=30$ dates.

The baseline includes every such date, even those without a feature value.
The baseline is therefore not simply the average of the bins. Each synthetic
history has its own baseline.

\section{Stage 2: comparison with the baseline}\label{sec:comparison}

Stage~2 asks how much a bin changes the chance of each touch.

\subsection{Baseline subtraction}

\begin{keyconcept}{The shift Surface}
\[
 Z_k(\delta,\theta)=Y_k(\delta,\theta)-B(\delta,\theta),
\]
\normalsize cell by cell. A cell blank in either Surface is blank in $Z_k$.
\end{keyconcept}
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[Positive cell (red)] The barrier was touched more often in the bin
       than over all dates.
 \item[Negative cell (blue)] The barrier was touched less often.
 \item[Zero cell (white)] The bin made no difference at that barrier and
       horizon.
\end{description}
Every synthetic history subtracts its own baseline, never the observed one.

\section{Stage 3: validation by null comparison}\label{sec:validation}

Stage~2 produced a shift Surface for every bin. Stage~3 asks one question
of each: \emph{could a market in which the feature cannot matter have
produced a Surface this lopsided?} It condenses each Surface into one score,
builds $I=1{,}000$ synthetic histories that have no memory, scores the same
bin on each, and counts how often a synthetic history scores at least as high.

After the score is defined (Section~\ref{sec:score}), fix one feature and one
bin and drop the bin index. Write $Z$ for the bin's observed shift Surface,
$Q_{\mathrm{obs}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{obs}}$ for its observed score and support
flag, and $Q_i$ and $V_i$ for the same quantities on synthetic history
$i=1,\ldots,I$.

\begin{keyconcept}{Stage 3 at a glance}
\normalsize
\begin{align*}
 \text{observed history}\quad
   & \xrightarrow{\text{Stages 1--2}}Z
     \xrightarrow{\text{score}}Q_{\mathrm{obs}}\\
 \text{synthetic histories } 1,\ldots,I\quad
   & \xrightarrow{\text{Stages 1--2}}Z_1,\ldots,Z_I
     \xrightarrow{\text{score}}Q_1,\ldots,Q_I\\
 \text{compare}\quad
   & \xrightarrow{\text{how often }Q_i\geq Q_{\mathrm{obs}}}\widehat p
\end{align*}
\end{keyconcept}

\subsection{Scoring a shift Surface}\label{sec:score}

The score measures how differently a condition moves the chance of an
up-move and a down-move of the same size. Each barrier is compared with its
mirror image at the same horizon, $+\delta$ with $-\delta$. In this subsection
the bin index $k$ stays, because which cells count depends on the other bins
of the same feature.

Only barriers with a mirror can be compared:
\[
 \Delta_\pm=\{\delta\in\Delta:\ \delta\neq0,\ \text{both }\delta\text{ and }-\delta\text{ appear exactly once in }\Delta\},
 \qquad
 \delta_{\max}=\max_{\delta\in\Delta_\pm}|\delta|.
\]
\begin{keyconcept}{The score: asymmetry between mirror barriers}
\[
 Q_k=\sum_{(\delta,\theta)\in\mathcal U_k}
   \frac{|\delta|}{2\,\delta_{\max}}\,
   \bigl|Z_k(\delta,\theta)-Z_k(-\delta,\theta)\bigr|,
 \qquad
 V_k=\ind\bigl[\mathcal U_k\neq\varnothing\bigr],
\]
\normalsize where $\mathcal U_k\subseteq\Delta_\pm\times\Theta$ is the set of
usable cells of bin $k$, defined below.
\end{keyconcept}
Read the formula one piece at a time:
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[Mirror difference] $|Z_k(\delta,\theta)-Z_k(-\delta,\theta)|$ is large
       when the condition makes one side more likely to be touched and the
       other side less likely. If both sides shift by the same amount, it is
       zero. The absolute value ignores which side wins.
 \item[Distance weight] $|\delta|/\delta_{\max}$ makes far barriers count
       more, in proportion to their distance: a $\pm2\%$ pair counts twice
       as much as a $\pm1\%$ pair.
 \item[The factor $1/2$] Every pair appears twice in the sum, once at
       $+\delta$ and once at $-\delta$. Halving counts each pair once.
\end{description}

\topic{Which cells count}

A cell is \emph{usable} for bin $k$ when three things hold at that barrier and
horizon: bin $k$ has at least $n_{\min}=30$ dates, both mirror shifts exist,
and at least one other bin of the same feature meets the same two conditions.
First collect the bins that are measurable at a mirror pair,
\[
 \mathcal L(\delta,\theta)=\bigl\{j:\ N_j(\theta)\geq n_{\min},\
   Z_j(\delta,\theta)\text{ and }Z_j(-\delta,\theta)\text{ are not blank}\bigr\},
 \qquad\delta\in\Delta_\pm,
\]
then keep the cells where bin $k$ is one of at least two such bins:
\begin{keyconcept}{Usable cells}
\[
 \mathcal U_k=\bigl\{(\delta,\theta)\in\Delta_\pm\times\Theta:\
   k\in\mathcal L(\delta,\theta),\ |\mathcal L(\delta,\theta)|\geq2\bigr\}.
\]
\end{keyconcept}
A cell therefore counts only where the feature splits the dates into at least
two measurable groups. The zero barrier is not in $\Delta_\pm$ and never counts.

A bin is \emph{supported} ($V_k=1$) when at least one of its cells is usable.
An unsupported bin is stored with score $0$, but ``unsupported'' and ``scored
zero'' are different facts: a supported bin whose mirror shifts are equal
also scores $0$. Losing some horizons does not make a bin unsupported; only
losing every usable cell does.

The grid should be symmetric. A barrier outside $\Delta_\pm$ is ignored, and
it does not affect $\delta_{\max}$: adding an unpaired $+5\%$ row to a
$\pm2\%$ grid leaves every weight unchanged. A grid with $\Delta_\pm=\varnothing$
gives every bin score $0$ and no support. That outcome is a configuration
mistake, not evidence of no effect.

\topic{What the score does not do}

The score is a weighted sum of probability differences, not a probability,
and it can exceed one. It is not divided by the number of usable cells, so a
bin with more usable cells can reach a larger score. Every usable cell
counts equally whether it rests on 31 dates or 3,000. For scale, a rate
measured from 30 independent dates has a standard error of about $0.09$
near $50\%$, and overlapping forward windows make the real uncertainty
larger. The score does not weight cells by this precision.

\subsection{The synthetic-history model}\label{sec:null}

A synthetic history should look like the market one bar at a time, but have
no memory: nothing in past bars can say anything about the next one. Then
no feature, whether computed from past bars or taken from outside data, can
predict the next bar, so any score it
earns is chance.

Each bar is described by four log components:
\begin{center}
\large
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c l@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{ConceptPaper}
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Part of the bar} \\
\midrule
$g^{\mathrm{open}}_t$ & $\log\left(\dfrac{\Open_t}{\Close_{t-1}}\right)$ & Gap from the previous close \\
$g^{\mathrm{close}}_t$ & $\log\left(\dfrac{\Close_t}{\Open_t}\right)$ & Body, open to close \\
$g^{\mathrm{high}}_t$ & $\log\left(\dfrac{\High_t}{\max(\Open_t,\Close_t)}\right)$ & Upper wick \\
$g^{\mathrm{low}}_t$ & $\log\left(\dfrac{\Low_t}{\min(\Open_t,\Close_t)}\right)$ & Lower wick \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Stack them into one vector per bar,
$g_t=(g^{\mathrm{open}}_t,g^{\mathrm{close}}_t,g^{\mathrm{high}}_t,g^{\mathrm{low}}_t)\in\mathbb R^4$.
Let $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ be the sample mean and covariance of the observed
vectors, then draw every synthetic bar on its own:
\begin{keyconcept}{One synthetic bar: realistic shape, no memory}
\[
 g_1,\ldots,g_{|\mathcal T|}\ \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\ \mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma).
\]
\end{keyconcept}
Within a bar the four components keep their observed correlations; across
bars they are independent. Chain the bars into prices, starting from the
first observed close as $\Close_0$:
\[
 \Open_t=\Close_{t-1}\,e^{g^{\mathrm{open}}_t},\qquad
 \Close_t=\Open_t\,e^{g^{\mathrm{close}}_t},
\]
\[
 \High_t=\max(\Open_t,\Close_t)\,e^{g^{\mathrm{high}}_t},\qquad
 \Low_t=\min(\Open_t,\Close_t)\,e^{g^{\mathrm{low}}_t}.
\]
Each synthetic history has as many bars as the observed one, and volume is
copied from the observed history. A fixed seed makes the ensemble
reproducible.
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[What the null keeps] The average size of each part of a bar, and
       how the four parts move together within one bar.
 \item[What the null discards] Every link between bars: autocorrelation,
       volatility clustering, and regimes. It also has no liquidity,
       execution, or trading costs.
\end{description}

\topic{What this null can and cannot show}

Real markets have memory; this null has none. A feature that tracks
persistent volatility or a regime can therefore beat the null without
predicting anything beyond ``volatile periods stay volatile.'' Clearing
means \emph{beats a memoryless market}, not \emph{carries a usable edge}.

The normal draw can also produce impossible bars. An upper wick of $-1\%$
puts the high below the body; the reconstruction does not repair such bars.

A null with memory, such as a block bootstrap, would fix the first problem
but could also keep the very feature--future relationship under test. Any
replacement has to say what it removes, what it keeps, and how its
calibration is checked.

\subsection{Repeating measurement on each null path}

Features follow one of two policies on synthetic histories:
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[OHLCV-derived feature (recomputed)] Recompute the feature and its
       quantile edges on each synthetic history.
 \item[External, calendar, or workspace-extension feature (fixed)]
       These cannot be rebuilt from synthetic prices, so every synthetic
       history reuses the observed values and cutoffs.
\end{description}
Each synthetic history then goes through Stages~1--2 and the scoring above
exactly as the observed history did, giving $Q_1,\ldots,Q_I$. ``The same
bin'' means the same position, such as the lowest decile, on every history.
For a recomputed feature, its cutoffs are that history's own deciles, so
they differ between histories. Volume is copied from the observed history
even when a feature uses it, so its link to synthetic prices is lost.

\subsection{The test and decision rule}

The p-value is the observed score's rank among all $1+I$ scores:
\begin{keyconcept}{The Monte Carlo p-value and the decision}
\[
 \widehat p=\frac{1+\#\{i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}:\ Q_i\geq Q_{\mathrm{obs}}\}}{1+I},
 \qquad
 C=\ind\bigl[V_{\mathrm{obs}}=1\ \text{and}\ \widehat p<0.05\bigr].
\]
\normalsize The bin clears when $C=1$.
\end{keyconcept}
The $1$ in the numerator counts the observed history as one of the draws,
so $\widehat p$ is never zero; with $I=1{,}000$ the smallest possible value
is $1/1{,}001$. Ties count against the bin. Unsupported observed bins are
skipped, not tested. There is no multiple-testing correction in this rule;
Stage~4 reports one beside it.

\topic{Unsupported synthetic runs push $\widehat p$ down}

On some synthetic histories this bin is unsupported and scores $0$, which
can never reach a positive $Q_{\mathrm{obs}}$. Those runs stay in the count,
which makes $\widehat p$ smaller than if they were left out. The alternative
counts only the $M=\sum_{i=1}^{I}V_i$ runs on which the bin was supported:
\[
 \widehat p_{\mathrm{supported}}
 =\frac{1+\#\{i:\ V_i=1,\ Q_i\geq Q_{\mathrm{obs}}\}}{1+M}.
\]
The difference can decide the outcome. With $M=100$ supported runs:
\begin{center}
\large
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c c c@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{ConceptPaper}
\textbf{Supported runs} $\boldsymbol{\geq Q_{\mathrm{obs}}}$ & \textbf{Current} $\widehat p$ & $\boldsymbol{\widehat p_{\mathrm{supported}}}$ \\
\midrule
$4$ & \heatcell{ConceptPaper}{$5/1{,}001\approx0.0050$ clears} & \heatcell{ConceptPaper}{$5/101\approx0.0495$ clears} \\
$5$ & \heatcell{ConceptPaper}{$6/1{,}001\approx0.0060$ clears} & $6/101\approx0.0594$ does not \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Neither version is automatically right. The current rule compares against
every synthetic history; the alternative compares only against histories on
which this bin could be measured. The current rule is the implemented one,
and each result records $M$ so the gap can be checked. In both versions the
null is fitted to the observed history, so $\widehat p$ carries no exact
guarantee.

\topic{Clearing is not discovery}

At a $0.05$ threshold, about one bin in twenty clears by chance when it has
no effect. Among 1,000 tested bins with no effect at all, about 50 would
clear. Clearing is an exploratory flag. Stage~4 therefore reports the count
expected by chance and a q-value beside each bin
(Section~\ref{sec:selection}).

\section{Stage 4: selection and presentation}\label{sec:selection}

Stage~4 adds no new test. It keeps the bins that passed, orders them by
evidence, and says how much passing means when many bins are tested. From
here on, the bins of all features are pooled: a \emph{tested bin} $b$ is one
supported bin of one feature, with p-value $\widehat p_b$.

\subsection{Selection and rendering rules}

\begin{keyconcept}{Every bin that clears, and only those}
\[
 \mathcal K_{\mathrm{sel}}=\{b:\ C_b=1\}=\{b:\ V_b=1\ \text{and}\ \widehat p_b<0.05\},
\]
\normalsize where $b$ ranges over all tested bins.
\end{keyconcept}
Each kept bin is drawn as its shift Surface heatmap beside the histogram of
its 1,000 synthetic scores. Stage~3 draws the same figure for every tested
bin.

There is no top-$k$ cut, quota per feature, or minimum effect size. Kept
bins are ranked by $\widehat p$ alone; bins with equal $\widehat p$ share a
rank, and the score never breaks a tie. The order is evidence against
chance, not size of effect.

\topic{How much would pass by chance}

Let $m$ be the number of tested bins. Two numbers put the selection in
context; neither changes it.
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[Expected by chance] $0.05\,m$: how many bins would clear if none had
       any effect and the test were exactly calibrated.
 \item[q-value] The Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted p-value: roughly, the share
       of bins at least this strong that would be false discoveries.
\end{description}
Sort the p-values of all $m$ tested bins, not only the kept ones, as
$\widehat p_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\widehat p_{(m)}$. The bin at rank $j$ gets
\begin{keyconcept}{The q-value}
\[
 q_{(j)}=\min_{l\geq j}\ \min\Bigl(1,\ \frac{m\,\widehat p_{(l)}}{l}\Bigr).
\]
\normalsize The core is $\widehat p\times m/\text{rank}$; the minimum over
later ranks keeps $q$ from falling as $\widehat p$ rises, and bins with equal
$\widehat p$ share a $q$.
\end{keyconcept}
For example, with $m=400$ tested bins, a bin ranked 10th with
$\widehat p=0.01$ has $q\leq0.01\times400/10=0.4$: of the ten strongest bins,
about four would be expected by chance.

The q-value's guarantee assumes independent or positively related tests.
Here all features of one history share the same synthetic histories, and
the simulator's own calibration is not established (Section~\ref{sec:null}),
so q-values give context, not a guarantee.

The number of synthetic histories limits q-values. With $I=1{,}000$ the
smallest possible $\widehat p$ is $1/1{,}001$. If one of 400 tested bins
reaches it and every other bin has $\widehat p=0.5$, that bin's q-value is
$400/1{,}001\approx0.40$. The strongest possible single result is still
unremarkable at the family level.

\section{Stage 5: combination of active conditions}\label{sec:forecast}

Stage~5 adds no test and changes no selection. It asks what the kept
conditions imply together for the last stored bar, and how far that combined
estimate is from what history shows when those conditions held at once.

\begin{keyconcept}{Stage 5 at a glance}
\normalsize
\[
 \text{kept bins}
 \xrightarrow{\text{hold on the last bar}}
 \text{active}
 \xrightarrow{\text{one per family}}
 \text{used}
 \xrightarrow{\text{combine}}
 (P,\ J).
\]
\end{keyconcept}

\subsection{Active and used conditions}

Let $t_{\mathrm{last}}$ be the last stored bar.
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[Active bin] A kept bin $k$ of a feature whose value on the last bar
       falls in it: $x_{t_{\mathrm{last}}}$ is finite and
       $k(t_{\mathrm{last}})=k$, under the feature's stored
       Stage~1 edges.
 \item[Used condition] The best-ranked active bin within its feature family,
       with ties broken by feature and then bin. Active bins from a history
       whose dates differ from the baseline's are not used. Every active bin
       that is not used is listed with the reason.
\end{description}
Write $\mathcal C$ for the used conditions and $A_c$ for the dates on which
condition $c\in\mathcal C$ held.
Families group closely related features, such as one indicator at several
periods, so the one-per-family rule removes their most obvious overlap. It
does not remove overlap between different families.

\subsection{Naive Bayes combination}

At one barrier and horizon, let $E$ be the event of a touch. Bayes' rule
combines conditions through their odds:
\[
 \operatorname{odds}\Bigl(E\Bigm|{\textstyle\bigcap_{c\in\mathcal C}A_c}\Bigr)
 =\operatorname{odds}(E)
  \prod_{c\in\mathcal C}\frac{\operatorname{odds}(E\mid A_c)}{\operatorname{odds}(E)}.
\]
Each condition multiplies the baseline odds by how much it changes the odds
on its own. This is exact only when the conditions are independent once the
outcome is known.

Rates are smoothed first, as if each group had one extra touch and one extra
miss, so that a rate of $0$ or $1$ still has finite odds:
\[
 \tilde p=\frac{\text{touches}+1}{\text{dates}+2}.
\]
Taking logarithms turns the product into a sum. With
$\operatorname{logit}p=\log\bigl(p/(1-p)\bigr)$:
\begin{keyconcept}{Naive Bayes: each condition adds its log odds ratio}
\[
 \operatorname{logit}P(\delta,\theta)
 =\operatorname{logit}\tilde B(\delta,\theta)
  +\sum_{c\in\mathcal C}\bigl(\operatorname{logit}\tilde Y_c(\delta,\theta)-\operatorname{logit}\tilde B(\delta,\theta)\bigr),
\]
\normalsize so $P\in\mathcal S$, built cell by cell.
\end{keyconcept}
Its support is the weakest contributor at each horizon,
\[
 n^P(\theta)=\min\Bigl(N(\theta),\ \min_{c\in\mathcal C}N_c(\theta)\Bigr),
\]
and a cell of $P$ is blank when $n^P(\theta)<n_{\min}$. With no used
conditions, $P=\tilde B$. Conditions that measure the same thing count the same evidence
more than once, which pushes $P$ further from the baseline than the evidence
supports.

\subsection{The historical joint rate}

The direct estimate needs no independence assumption:
\begin{keyconcept}{The historical joint rate}
\[
 J=\Pi\Bigl[\,\bigcap_{c\in\mathcal C}A_c\Bigr]\in\mathcal S,
\]
\normalsize the unsmoothed touch rate over the dates on which \emph{every}
used condition held.
\end{keyconcept}
It is the same operator $\Pi$ that produced $Y_k$ and $B$, so it is blank
below $n_{\min}$ such dates. With no used conditions, the intersection is
all of $\mathcal T$ and $J=B$.

$P$ and $J$ trade one weakness for another:
\begin{description}[font=\normalfont\large\bfseries\color{ConceptInk}]
 \item[Naive Bayes Surface $P$] Uses each condition's full sample, but
       assumes the conditions are independent given the outcome.
 \item[Historical joint Surface $J$] Assumes nothing about dependence, but
       its sample shrinks quickly: two unrelated decile conditions hold
       together on about one date in a hundred.
\end{description}
The gap $P-J$, summarized by its average and its largest value, shows how far
the independence assumption moves the estimate, read against how few dates
$J$ rests on.

Both are in-sample. Every used bin was kept because its score was extreme,
so its measured shift is likely larger than its true effect, and neither
$P$ nor $J$ corrects for that.

\subsection{Nesting of touch events}

A farther barrier can only be reached by passing a nearer one on the same
side, and a touch within $\theta$ bars is also a touch within any longer
horizon. Any Surface $F$ of touch probabilities must therefore satisfy
\begin{keyconcept}{Nesting inequalities}
\begin{align*}
 F(\delta',\theta)&\leq F(\delta,\theta)&&\text{for }0\leq\delta<\delta'\text{ or }\delta'<\delta<0,\\
 F(\delta,\theta)&\leq F(\delta,\theta')&&\text{for }\theta<\theta'.
\end{align*}
\end{keyconcept}
The measured Surface of Section~\ref{sec:example} respects this; a combined
Surface need not:
\begin{center}
\large
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l c c@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{ConceptPaper}
\textbf{Barrier} & \textbf{Nested (measured $Y_k$)} & \textbf{Violating (illustrative)} \\
\midrule
$0\%$  & \heatcell[white]{SurfaceRed!80!SurfaceAmber}{$0.90$} & \heatcell[white]{SurfaceRed!80!SurfaceAmber}{$0.90$} \\
$+1\%$ & \heatcell{SurfaceAmber!90!white}{$0.45$} & \heatcell{SurfaceAmber!90!white}{$0.45$} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In the right column the $+2\%$ barrier is more likely to be touched than the
nearer $+1\%$ barrier, which is impossible for real touch probabilities.

Stage~5 counts the adjacent pairs of cells of $P$ that break these
inequalities: neighbouring barriers within $\delta<0$, neighbouring barriers
within $\delta\geq0$, and neighbouring horizons, ignoring blank cells. It
reports the count without correcting it. Adding log odds ratios cell by cell
does not have to preserve the order. Even measured rates can break the
horizon order slightly, because longer horizons leave out the most recent
dates.

\subsection{Strongest cell of a used condition}

For each used condition, the report also points to one cell worth looking
at: the largest shift inside a run of at least two neighbouring barrier rows
that all move the same way by at least 10 percentage points, at a horizon
where the bin has at least 50 dates (the NASDAQ defaults). It is a reading
aid for the shift Surface; nothing is tested.

condition 1 has 40 among 100, and condition 2 has 35 among 100, from
different families. Smoothing and log-odds give
